When a company faces a claim it expects to lose, the primary risk is rarely the individual refund or damages owed to a single subscriber—it is the systemic fallout of losing in an open courtroom. Routing the claim into individual arbitration contains that damage in four concrete ways:

    No Binding Legal Precedent: Unlike a published court ruling—which establishes legal precedent that other consumers, attorneys, and regulators can immediately cite—an arbitrator's decision applies only to that single claimant and carries zero preclusive weight in future cases.

    Elimination of Class-Action Exposure: Because consumer arbitration agreements pair mandatory arbitration with class-action waivers, losing to one subscriber only requires paying that single user's individual damages (for instance, a few months of subscription fees) rather than multiplying liability across millions of subscribers.

    Private Proceedings & Sealed Discovery: Arbitration hearings are closed to the public, and internal documents or pricing/quota formulas produced during the process are routinely shielded by protective orders or settlement NDAs—preventing internal metrics from becoming part of a searchable public court docket.

    High Friction Deterrence: Requiring every dissatisfied subscriber to file an individual arbitration notice, pay initial filing fees (even if later reimbursed), and litigate one-on-one ensures that only a tiny fraction of affected users will ever pursue a claim to completion.

In short, losing a single private arbitration costs a company a small, isolated payout with no public record and no ripple effect—whereas losing the exact same issue in public court creates precedent, exposes internal records, and opens the door to class-wide liability.
