In U.S. law, jurisdiction over a domestic corporation is territorial and statutory—not optional—so a company cannot place itself beyond government authority. However, history does record a few well-known scenarios where corporations deliberately structured their operations to evade specific regulatory oversight until courts or Congress shut down the loophole:

    Regulatory Arbitrage & Offshore / Tribal-Charter Lending Schemes (2000s–2010s): Certain online payday lenders partnered with Native American tribes or offshore entities to claim tribal sovereign immunity or foreign jurisdiction, intentionally attempting to place their high-interest consumer loans outside state usury laws and state Attorney General enforcement. Federal courts (such as in Otoe-Missouria Tribe v. New York State Department of Financial Services) and the CFPB rejected those shields when non-tribal corporations controlled the lending operations, ruling that a private company cannot rent sovereign immunity to escape state or federal jurisdiction.

    The "Swaps Loophole" / Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000 (Enron & OTC Derivatives): Following aggressive corporate lobbying, Congress passed the CFMA of 2000, which explicitly barred both the CFTC and the SEC from regulating over-the-counter (OTC) energy and credit derivatives. Firms like Enron and AIG knowingly operated multi-trillion-dollar trading markets inside that statutory blind spot—not because they sat outside U.S. sovereignty, but because Congress had affirmatively stripped regulators of authority—until the 2008 financial crisis led to the Dodd-Frank Act closing the exemption.

    "Flags of Convenience" & Offshore Tax/Maritime Structuring: U.S.-headquartered cruise lines and shipping companies routinely incorporate vessels in Panama, Liberia, or the Bahamas to avoid U.S. labor, tax, and maritime inspection rules. Even there, federal courts still exercise jurisdiction whenever those operations market to U.S. consumers or touch U.S. ports.

In every historical case, whenever a corporation claimed or engineered a "jurisdiction-free" zone for consumer transactions, U.S. courts and legislatures eventually treated that structure as either unlawful regulatory evasion or a temporary statutory gap to be closed—never as a valid legal precedent permitting unaccountable commercial operation.
