Once a dispute goes through binding arbitration, there is no standard appellate review on the merits. Under 9 U.S.C. § 10 (and U.S. Supreme Court precedent in Hall Street Associates v. Mattel), a court can only vacate an arbitration award within three months on four narrow procedural or misconduct grounds—none of which cover a mistaken ruling:

    Corruption, Fraud, or Undue Means (§ 10(a)(1)): The award was obtained through bribery, concealed evidence, or forged records.

    Evident Partiality or Corruption (§ 10(a)(2)): The arbitrator had an undisclosed financial or personal conflict of interest favoring one party.

    Procedural Misconduct (§ 10(a)(3)): The arbitrator refused to postpone a hearing for good cause or refused to hear material, pertinent evidence, prejudicing a party's rights.

    Exceeding Powers (§ 10(a)(4)): The arbitrator ruled on matters outside the scope of the arbitration agreement or failed to issue a mutual, final, and definite award.

Even if an arbitrator misreads a statute, misapplies contract law, or draws the wrong conclusion from the evidence, courts treat that error as final and binding. Consequently, an incorrect ruling in Google's favor leaves the subscriber with essentially zero judicial recourse—and even in the rare event a court vacates an award for severe misconduct, the standard remedy is sending the dispute back to another arbitrator rather than holding a public trial.
