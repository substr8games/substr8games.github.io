Q: What is the product being sold?
A: Restricted/rate limited access to its frontier AI models (Gemini, Gemini Flash, Gemini Argon, Gemini Hydrogen, etc.)

Q: Is a business required, or have businesses historically been required, to define the terms of a restricted/rate limited product?
A: Yes — both historically and under current consumer protection standards, businesses selling restricted, capped, or metered services are required to clearly disclose material limitations prior to purchase. (Appendix A)

Q: Does the public have a legal right to know the details of the product they are purchasing, including the terms of a paid monthly subscription (contract)?
A: Yes — under federal and state consumer protection law, consumers have an established legal right to clear, conspicuous disclosure of all material terms of a recurring subscription contract prior to purchase. (Appendix B)

Q: Does Google continue to offer the product to the public without regard for established law, historical precedent, and without regard to the customer's vested interest in knowing what contract they are agreeing to?
A: Yes — in practice, Google continues to market and sell its consumer AI subscription tiers without publishing fixed numerical usage quotas, relying instead on relative multipliers and dynamic compute-based limits that can change without prior notice. (Appendix C)

Q: Is a paid member of the service contractually routed into arbitration at Google's discretion regardless of whether it is established that Google's business practices do not follow Federal Law, California State Law, or Contract Law?
A: In practice, yes — unless the subscriber opted out within 30 days or qualifies for a narrow statutory or small-claims exception, a paid member is contractually routed into private individual arbitration even when alleging violations of federal law, California law, or contract law. (Appendix D)

Q: Does Google have a vested interest in routing a complaint into arbitration if the result of the arbitration is likely to work out in favor of the claimant?
A: Yes — even if an individual claimant is likely to win on the merits, a large corporation has a strong structural and financial interest in routing that dispute into private arbitration rather than public court. (Appendix E)

Q: Does Google have a vested interest in routing a complaint into arbitration if the result of the arbitration is likely to work out in favor of Google?
A: Yes — even when a company expects to win on the merits, it retains a clear procedural and economic interest in routing the dispute into private arbitration rather than civil court. (Appendix F)

Q: Does a paid member have any recourse should Google's arbitration lead to an incorrect conclusion that results in Google's favor?
A: Practically no — under the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA), an arbitrator getting the facts or the law wrong is not grounds to overturn or appeal the decision. (Appendix G)

Q: Does Google offer the public access to the paid product any other way than through a contract which routes the claimant into Google's arbitration?
A: For consumer subscriptions in the United States, no — every retail purchase channel routes the subscriber into the same standardized clickwrap Terms of Service containing the mandatory arbitration clause. (Appendix H)

Q: Does Google effectively operate outside of any legal jurisdiction with regard to the paid product?
A: Yes — specifically with regard to the paid consumer product's compute quotas, throttling, and model substitution, Google operates in an effective enforcement vacuum where no regulatory body or court is actively compelling compliance with pre-sale disclosure rules. (Appendix I)

Q: Is there historical or legal precedent for a corporation to operate effectively outside any legal jurisdiction or regulatory authority in the United States, under any conditions?
A: No — there is no historical or legal precedent in the United States granting a private domestic corporation blanket immunity from all legal jurisdiction or regulatory authority. (Appendix J)
